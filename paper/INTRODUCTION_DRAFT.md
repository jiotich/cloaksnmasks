Online advertisements do more than present a message: they provide a path from a platform’s feed to an off-platform destination. The text shown in an ad, the address embedded in its link, and the content ultimately reached after navigation can be related but are not necessarily identical. Shorteners, tracking services, affiliate redirects, dynamic creatives, and geographic routing all complicate the relationship between a displayed ad and its destination. These mechanisms have ordinary uses, but they can also make the destination difficult for a viewer—or an auditor—to infer from a static ad snapshot.

Cloaking and deceptive redirection have a long history in web measurement and security. WWW research studied search-engine cloaking, while later work examined conditional content served to machines and users and the misuse of ad-supported URL-shortening services. Invernizzi et al. explicitly studied cloaking affecting Google Search and Google Ads, making it inappropriate to frame this paper as the first study of advertising cloaking generally. Research on Meta/Facebook advertising has separately developed methods for auditing the Ad Library, measuring political advertising, analyzing ad delivery, and characterizing problematic advertising. Work at WWW, NDSS, USENIX Security, FAccT, and ICWSM establishes a substantial literature on Meta ads and the transparency environment. What motivates this study is a narrower question: how do the URLs embedded in a large Meta-ad sample behave, and how do observable URL-related signals relate to the ads’ creative and available ad metadata?

We study a Brazil-focused collection of Meta Ad Library records gathered from April 20 to June 19, 2026. The current manuscript reports **[verified number of unique ad records]** ads after the collection, deduplication, and parse-coverage audit. The dataset contains ad metadata and creative assets; the project also has an image-classification effort reported as approximately 320,000 images, whose asset-to-ad linkage and coverage must be reported separately. We analyze each ad’s embedded link fields and snapshot-level creative structure, and we use geolocated URL-probe results where the requested URL can be matched to a collected ad URL. The analysis is limited to the population and fields actually recovered; it is not a census of all Meta ads or an observation of Meta’s internal review decisions.

We organize the study around three questions. **RQ1 asks how destination URLs in the collected ads are structured and distributed across domains, public suffixes, advertisers, and ad formats.** This characterization establishes the units and baselines needed for interpreting individual patterns: link occurrences, exact URLs, ads, advertisers, and registered domains are reported separately. It also allows us to distinguish the concentration of links on a small set of destinations from the number of distinct advertisers using those destinations.

**RQ2 asks what observable URL-related cloaking signals occur and how prevalent they are.** We examine differences between URL-like caption text and the registered domain of an embedded link, heterogeneity among the destination domains in card-based ads, and variation in observed final destinations for the same requested URL across the available probes. We treat these as candidate signals rather than automatic evidence of maliciousness. A displayed-domain mismatch may be benign or may be a shortened, tracked, or affiliate URL; a carousel may legitimately link to several products; and country-dependent destinations may reflect localization or testing. The probe files contain observed final URLs and request errors, not a complete hop-by-hop redirect trace. Accordingly, a count of final destinations is not a redirect-chain length, and geographic variation alone does not demonstrate that Meta’s reviewers and users received different content. Meta’s public policies discuss cloaking and deceptive links, but this study’s measurements are observational indicators, not adjudications of policy violations.

**RQ3 asks how ads with at least one RQ2 candidate signal differ from other ads in image-label categories and the ad metadata present in the collection.** We link image-level classifications to ads using the verified media-hash-to-ad relation, report image-level and ad-level coverage separately, and aggregate multiple labeled assets to ad-level label presence. Missing labels are treated as missing, not as negative examples. We also compare publisher platform, page/advertiser categories, page entity type, call to action, and active dates/lifetime when those values exist. For every field, we report non-missing coverage in the analyzed database; raw active-date fields are preserved, and a date-derived lifetime is calculated only when both dates parse and are chronologically consistent. The field `total_active_time` is retained as recorded rather than assigned an undocumented unit.

The paper makes three focused contributions, conditional on the final data and validation audit:

1. **A URL-centered characterization of a Meta-ad sample**, with explicit units, coverage, and destination-domain distributions.
2. **A reproducible measurement of URL-related candidate signals**, separating internal ad fields from probe-based outcomes and separating observable variation from claims about intent or platform-review evasion.
3. **An ad-level comparison of candidate-signal ads and other ads**, combining the existing image classifications with the metadata fields demonstrably available in the collection.

The result is a platform-specific view of how the links in Meta ads are represented and where they lead, alongside the limits of what a transparency snapshot and destination probes can show. We do not infer malicious intent from a URL pattern alone, and we do not claim a reviewer/user split-view without a direct controlled comparison. The remainder of the paper describes the collection and measurement decisions, presents the URL characterization and cloaking-signal results in RQ order, compares ad-level image and metadata features, and discusses interpretation, limitations, and implications for independent ad auditing.
